Group E at Uaxactún, in the Petén lowlands of northern Guatemala (about 17.4° N, roughly 19 km north of Tikal), is one of the most famous architectural complexes in the study of Maya astronomy. On its western side stands Structure E-VII-sub, a radial pyramid with stairways on all four sides. Facing it across an open plaza, on the eastern side, a single long north–south platform supports three smaller buildings: E-I to the north, E-II in the centre and E-III to the south, with the two outer buildings equidistant from the central one. Frans Blom mapped the group in 1924, and Oliver Ricketson first proposed in 1928 that it was an astronomically oriented complex, during the Carnegie Institution excavations of 1926–1937. Because Uaxactún was the first such complex to be described, archaeologists now call every building group with this layout an "E Group", and dozens are known across the Maya lowlands.

The classic interpretation is that an observer standing on the western pyramid and looking east would see the Sun rise over the northern building (E-I) at the June solstice, over the central building (E-II) at the equinoxes, and over the southern building (E-III) at the December solstice. Field measurements by Anthony Aveni and Horst Hartung (1989) supported this: as seen from a point just above the middle stairway of E-VII-sub, the Sun would have risen over the three eastern buildings at the June solstice, the equinoxes and the December solstice respectively. Aveni also notes that Group E as a whole is aligned to the cardinal directions. This is the reason Group E became famous, and it is the arrangement the lesson shows.

The underlying astronomy is well established and can be calculated. Because the Earth's axis is tilted, the point on the horizon where the Sun rises shifts during the year. At Uaxactún's latitude, on a flat horizon, the Sun rises furthest to the north-east around the June solstice (an azimuth of about 65°), due east around the equinoxes (90°), and furthest to the south-east around the December solstice (about 115°). Over a full year the sunrise point therefore travels back and forth along an arc of roughly 50° of the eastern horizon, pausing briefly at each end around the solstices before turning back. Anyone who watched sunrises from the same spot for a year could observe this pattern without instruments, and a row of buildings on the horizon could serve as fixed reference points for it.

E-VII-sub itself is an archaeological discovery. When the badly ruined later pyramid, Structure E-VII, was excavated, archaeologists found this earlier, well-preserved building inside it. E-VII-sub dates to the Late Preclassic period (Aveni gives roughly 0–200 CE). It was covered in white lime plaster and decorated with sixteen large stucco masks flanking its four stairways; Michael Coe has suggested the masks may represent the jaguar god of the underworld and sky serpents, an iconographic reading rather than a certainty. The standardisation of the Group E plan coincides with the period (around 150–250 CE) when Maya chiefdoms were developing into states, and burials and caches found at many E Groups suggest that their open plazas also became places where the emerging ruling elite performed ancestral rituals.

Scholars still debate how Group E was used. Aveni himself suggests it may be better understood as performative rather than practical: less a scientific laboratory than a public stage, more like a planetarium than an observatory, where people gathered to watch the Sun arrive at its appointed place during seasonal ceremonies. He also proposes that the precise solar arrangement at Uaxactún may have been short-lived, and that at more than a dozen later E Groups in the Petén the design was reworked to mark 20-day periods around the planting season. Ivan Šprajc (2021) is more sceptical: after studying the construction sequence, he argues that the classic sightlines connect buildings from different periods (the eastern platform and its outer buildings reached their final form in the Early Classic, after the earliest phases of the western pyramid had been covered), and that E Groups in general were not specialised observatories. Other scholars, including James Aimers and Prudence Rice, interpret E Groups primarily as ritual and public spaces whose layout referred symbolically to the solar cycle.

The academically strongest summary is therefore that Group E at Uaxactún is an architectural complex built around the movement of the Sun along the horizon, that measurements from E-VII-sub show the three eastern buildings marking the solstice and equinox sunrises, and that it became the model for an entire class of Maya buildings. The yearly north–south journey of the sunrise point is a real, observable phenomenon, and the Maya clearly paid close attention to the Sun's cycle. Whether Group E was used as a working observatory, as a ceremonial stage, or as both remains a subject of scholarly discussion. This distinction allows the lesson to show the famous alignment clearly, while separating archaeological evidence, astronomical calculation and scholarly interpretation.
